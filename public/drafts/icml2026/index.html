Concepts

Diffusion Language Models

Diffusion models are a class of models very used today for generation of unstructured data such as audio or image. TODO: explain standard diffusion models.

Apparently, they are also marginally used for language. The way they work is that they “denoise” a list of fully masked tokens, in no specifically predefined order. While I do not know how well they work in practice, dropping the constraint of generating the tokens left to right sounds very useful for translation-like tasks.

Talks

Talk: Shopify/Meta recommender systems

Two talks from Shopify and Meta people about modern recommender systems. While there was a lot of uninteresting details, the overall takeaway was interesting. Their point was that recommender systems nowadays are really more about the systems than the model/algorithm. Indeed, all individual components (recommendation algorithms, bandits algorithm, RL-based live updates etc.) are already efficient and well-understood and that most the complexity stems from the interplay between them, i.e. the overall pipeline.

Talk: Hibiku-Zero, a simultaneous Speech-to-Speech Translation Model Trained Without Aligned Data

The paper is by Kyutai, an AI lab doing a lot of very cool things in Paris.

The problem they’re trying to solve is automatic and no-latency2 voice translation. Their goal is to do all of that online while maintaining meaningful intonations into the generated speech translation, which is definitely ambitious. They showed us some demos, available here TODO: add link, which I found highly convincing.

The first interesting idea they used was for data generation: they started with only a dataset of recorded sentences in English. Then, they applied successively Speech-to-Text, Text-to-Text translation and Text-to-Speech strong models. There are two surprising elements with this approchac:

  • The fact that the Text to Speech was convincing enough that they got some good intonations out of that. This may just be me being unaware of how much these models improved recently in that aspect.
  • Sentence-level aligned data is way too coarse to train a model without latency. I will come back to that.

For the model architecture, they avoided using text as an intermediate representation and directly output audio tokens. Indeed, this is faster and retains more fidelity with respect to the intonations in the voice. After having done all of that, they trained the model and unsurprisingly: a lot of latency. Now to teach the model to do it faster, they did… Reinforcement Learning. The reward function was basically a rolling average of the ratio between the BLEU score3 of the partial translations against the complete correct translation. Because this is a rolling average, the earlier you get to a certain point in your translation, the more time you have to accumulate rewards, and thus the model is incentivized to translate faster.

Note 1: Among the technical details, one of the most surprising thing they did was injecting random “silence” tokens among the audio pairs in the datasets, at different spots. Apparently that helped the model generalize better during the reinforcement learning part.

Note 2: they also show it is possible to finetune the model on a new language with only about 1000 hours of new audio data.

Note 3: While it probably does not improve performance I wonder if it is possible to replace the BLEU metric with some cosine similarity of text embeddings, or if incomplete sentences completely break the text-embedding models.

Poster: Trainable Nonexpansive Denoisers for Contractive Image Reconstruction

TODO check paper to verify interpretation\

Given a noisy image $I$ of size $n \times m$ and a permutation $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n \times m}$ that preserves some amount of locality mostly, they train a neural network $\mathcal{N}$ to predict a weight matrix $W$ of size $n \times m$, from inputs $I$ and $\sigma(I)$. The point is to have $W_{i, j} \approx 1$ when the pixels $I_{i, j}$ and $\sigma(I)_{i, j}$ are very similar and close to zero otherwise.

We can then retrieve the unnoisy image by computing $I^* = \frac{1}{N}\sum_\sigma \sigma^{-1}(N(I, \sigma(I)) \odot \sigma(I))$ where the sum is taken over $N$ ($\approx 200$ permutations is already enough to get strong results)
